Act I · 1895

Every new medium
is born misunderstood

1895 · Paris

The Lumière brothers invent the cinematograph

The first filmmakers had zero patterns to copy — so they copied the thing they knew: theater.

One wide shot. Front-row seat. Four static minutes. Actors overacting for the back row. A play, but silent, flat, and gray.

"A world without sound, without color… the gray silhouettes of people, as though condemned to eternal silence… glide noiselessly along the gray ground, their smiles lifeless." Maxim Gorky, on seeing a Lumière film · 1896

1902 · The first jump cut in history

Méliès stops imitating theater

A magician named Georges Méliès discovers the cut — and A Trip to the Moon plays with time, space, and perspective in ways a stage never could.

The lesson, verbatim

"It wasn't trying to be theater.

It was taking advantage of the fact that it was not a play."

— Jack Conte, SXSW 2026

Act II

Every new medium
creates a panic class

Before the art arrives, the fear arrives first. It has a name every time. Last century it was "canned music." Today we call it "slop."

The pattern · four panics, one shape

1896

Film arrives. Gorky calls it a gray, lifeless shadow of theater.

1927

The Vitaphone. The Jazz Singer. Attendance explodes 50M → 90M/week — and theaters pay for the machines by firing their orchestras. 22,000 musicians lose their jobs by 1930. Soviet filmmakers declare sound will "destroy all its present formal achievements."

1930s

Recording. AFM president James Petrillo says recorded music is stealing 60% of live jobs. Launches a years-long national ban on recording — "canned music" ads show a robot feeding instruments into a meat grinder.

1939

The Novachord. First commercial synth — 163 vacuum tubes, 500 lbs, "virtually an orchestra in itself." The union bans it immediately.

The union's fears weren't stupid. In 1941, every #1 Billboard song was recorded by an orchestra. Today: essentially none.

But here's what the panic never predicts

The fear was real

Orchestral work genuinely collapsed. The "cost disease of the performing arts" (Baumol) meant a string quartet can't get more efficient — and the synth addressed that brutally. By 1991 the orchestra industry was in declared financial crisis; a 2008 Stanford GSB study found 46 of the 63 largest US orchestras running deficits.

The art was bigger

Thirty years after "canned music" ads, the Beatles made Revolver — looping tape, playing sounds backwards, running vocals through Leslie speakers. George Martin called the studio "a new canvas." The Beatles, Stevie Wonder, Prince, Madonna, Radiohead, Björk, Daft Punk — the banned instrument became the vocabulary of modern music.

Banned in panic → cherished as art. It keeps happening.

Act III · The man who refused

Charlie Chaplin
said no for 13 years

1927 → 1940

The greatest silent artist on Earth hated talkies

Chaplin "loathed them." He felt sound was spoiling the art of pantomime he'd spent a lifetime perfecting.

1931 — four years after The Jazz Singer — he releases City Lights with no synchronized dialogue. Then Modern Times, nearly a decade into the talkie era, still almost silent.

Others didn't survive the shift: John Gilbert's delivery made audiences laugh. Clara Bow — the "It Girl" — broke under it; her career was over by 1933.

"I wonder if Charlie felt conflicted about it… if he felt like he was choosing between being left behind and becoming irrelevant, or changing his work in a way that he was angry about and didn't believe in." Jack Conte · SXSW 2026

1940 · His first talkie

When Chaplin finally spoke, he didn't make small talk.

With Europe under occupation and America still neutral, The Great Dictator ends with Chaplin breaking character — speaking directly to camera, to the whole listening world.

What he said then is what needs saying now.
Here it is. All of it. His words, not ours.

The Great Dictator · 1940 · Verbatim · I of VIII

"I'm sorry, but I don't want to be an emperor. That's not my business. I don't want to rule or conquer anyone. I should like to help everyone if possible — Jew, Gentile, black man, white."

Verbatim · II of VIII

"We all want to help one another. Human beings are like that. But we have lost the way."

Verbatim · III of VIII

"Machinery that gives abundance has left us in want. Our knowledge has made us cynical. Our cleverness hard and unkind. We think too much and feel too little. More than machinery, we need humanity. More than cleverness, we need kindness and gentleness."

Verbatim · IV of VIII

"The airplane and the radio have brought us closer together. The very nature of these inventions cries out for the goodness in men."

Verbatim · V of VIII

"Even now my voice is reaching millions throughout the world — millions of despairing men, women, and little children. To those who can hear me, I say: do not despair. The hate of men will pass, and dictators die, and the power they took from the people will return to the people. And so long as men die, liberty will never perish."

Verbatim · VI of VIII

"Soldiers — don't give yourselves to brutes. Men who despise you, enslave you, who regiment your lives, tell you what to do, what to think, and what to feel. Who drill you, diet you, treat you like cattle, use you as cannon fodder. Don't give yourselves to these unnatural men — machine men with machine minds and machine hearts."

Verbatim · VII of VIII

"You are not machines. You are not cattle. You are men."

Verbatim · VIII of VIII

"You, the people, have the power — the power to create machines, the power to create happiness. You, the people, have the power to make this life free and beautiful, to make this life a wonderful adventure. Then, in the name of democracy, let us use that power — let us all unite."

Why we read all of it

He waited thirteen years to speak.
When he did, he spent it on us.

"Machine men with machine minds" was the warning.
"The power to create machines" was the assignment.
We are the generation that has to do both at once.

Act IV

Change is not death.
Change is a sine wave.

One band · twenty years · every wave

The Pomplamoose story is all of our stories

Jack's honesty about the dip: "I felt like I had failed… that I was unable to build this thing that persisted beyond technological changes."

And the recovery: "It worked… and it's when I learned a very important thing as an artist — change does not mean death. You can get back up and you can go again."

The part nobody puts on a poster

"Happily ever after is not a real place.

You figure out a model. New tech breaks it. You rebuild. It breaks again. You rebuild again. It's an infinite sine wave."

Which means the skill was never the tool

The durable skill is
rebuilding

Tools come and go on a 2–5 year cycle now.
The people who thrive are the ones who can rebuild without losing themselves.

Act V · 2026

We are in the dip.
And this time the sine wave is eating every industry at once.

Name the moment honestly

The "slop" phase is a phase — but the theft is real

Every medium starts as imitation

Right now we use image generators to imitate hand-drawn art, video generators to imitate cameras, music generators to imitate bassists and drummers. "That's what slop is — the lazy phase after the introduction of a new medium, where we're all just trying to reproduce existing things."

And consent matters

Creators' work trained these models "without consent, without credit, and without compensation." If it's fair use — why do AI companies cut deals with Disney, Vox, Warner Music? "If it's legal to just use it, why pay?" Jack's demand: pay creators — not because the tech is bad, but because it's good.

The part we don't say out loud at conferences

The mass disruption has already started

22K

musicians lost their jobs to the Vitaphone in under three years — from one invention, in one industry, in 1930.

100%

of #1 songs were orchestral in 1941. Essentially zero today. Whole craft economies can fully turn over inside a career.

Now

the wave isn't hitting one craft. It's hitting writing, design, code, support, analysis, teaching — simultaneously.

The kids graduating into this, and the adults ten years from retirement,
are being asked to cross the same river — with no bridge.

Permission slip, from the stage at SXSW

It's okay to feel conflicted.

Angry and grateful. Terrified and excited. "I feel like I get to be alive in 1895 for the invention of the cinematograph." Social media sorts us into for-and-against camps. Resist the sort. Hold two opposing ideas at once — humans are capable of this.

Act VI

Why humans still
win

Three load-bearing reasons · straight from Jack

1 · Risk

Comedians operate outside the normal distribution. "Rock and roll is a risk — you risk being ridiculed" (Sing Street). If an LLM says something crazy, what's it risking? The hot-dog-fingers scene works because the Daniels put themselves on the line. Taylor's Version works because a human went to battle with the machine.

2 · Scarcity

"Art is scarcity. Art is not what's easy to make — it's what's hard" (Kerrydax Chang). Invisible-selfie-stick videos got millions of views… until everyone could make one. Easy isn't interesting. Hard is interesting. Resourcefulness, ingenuity, patience, practice.

3 · Connection

A plumbing tutorial is information. good kid, m.A.A.d city is Kendrick — his story, his pain, his Compton. "That record isn't special because of what it sounds like. Kendrick is what's special about that record." Some media is function. The rest is one consciousness reaching another.

The thought experiment that settles it

Train a model on everything recorded up to 1959 — and nothing after.

Does it invent the Beatles without John, Paul, George and Ringo?
Stevie? Prince? Nirvana? Biggie? Beyoncé? Daft Punk? Frank Ocean? Jacob Collier?

"Leaps like this require a spirit of rule-breaking and invention
that I haven't yet seen demonstrated by these models."

Pattern recognition is not the whole of human creativity. We feel. We're conscious. We sometimes straight-up ignore logic. "We are weird, complex creatures."

The thesis, in one sentence

The people who are creative-but-not-technical and the people who are technical-but-not-creative are two halves of the same future — and nobody has built the place where they learn to work as one.

Until now.

Two people, one missing room

The Creative

"I have the vision.
I can't build it."

Designers, writers, musicians, filmmakers, teachers. They feel the new medium in their bones but the toolchain — git, agents, models, terminals — was built to keep them out. Every wave of the sine hits them first and hardest.

The Technical

"I can build anything.
I'm not sure what's worth building."

Engineers, sysadmins, data folks. Fluent in the machinery, starved for taste, story, and judgment — the exact things pattern-recognition can't supply. The wave doesn't spare them either: their moat is evaporating too.

Apart: both obsolete. Together: the new medium's first native speakers.
